Twelve weeks of physics, ecology, detection, engineering choice, liability,
process, communication, and evidence transfer all point at one document:
the final project. This lecture is about the structure that document
needs, using a fictional worked example to show what "arguing from three
strands" actually looks like written down, not just described.

## The six moves a proposal has to make

A marker reading your proposal is checking for six things, in this order,
and a gap in any one of them is a gap a marker will find:

1. **Problem** — what specific road, road class, or site type, and what
   about it makes wildlife-vehicle collision a genuine issue there (not
   "kangaroos are a problem in Australia" — that's too broad to evaluate).
2. **Evidence** — at least three of this unit's strands (physics/injury
   severity, ecology/behaviour, detection/reporting bias, engineering
   effectiveness, liability, process, communication, evidence transfer),
   argued specifically, not gestured at.
3. **Mechanism** — why the evidence implies this particular intervention
   works, in causal terms.
4. **Intervention** — one primary measure. Not a bundle of five different
   things "for thoroughness" — a marker reading a five-measure bundle
   cannot tell which measure you actually think does the work.
5. **Implementation** — a realistic jurisdiction, an approval pathway
   through that jurisdiction's actual process, and separated initial versus
   ongoing costs.
6. **Evaluation** — what would count as evidence the intervention worked,
   distinguishing a process metric (did the fence get built, is the
   underpass being used) from an outcome metric (did collisions actually
   fall, adjusted for exposure per Week 3).

A word on the synthetic dataset used throughout this unit: it is a teaching
tool, not real-world data. Present a pattern found in it as "in this
teaching dataset..." — never as a real-world finding, and never as evidence
supporting a real jurisdiction's numbers.

## A worked example: weak version

The fictional example below concerns **Kestrel Pass Road — a fictional
road, used here only as a worked teaching example, not a real place.**

> *This proposal recommends fencing, an underpass, a rope bridge canopy
> crossing, dynamic LED warning signs, and a public awareness campaign
> along Kestrel Pass Road. Together, these measures will dramatically
> reduce kangaroo-vehicle collisions and set a new standard for wildlife
> road safety. Implementation should proceed as soon as possible.*

What's wrong, move by move: no site detail beyond a name (Problem — too
thin); no strand cited at all (Evidence — missing entirely); "dramatically
reduce" is asserted, not derived from a mechanism (Mechanism — missing);
five measures bundled together (Intervention — not scoped); no
jurisdiction, no approval pathway, no cost figures at all (Implementation
— missing); no evaluation plan of any kind (Evaluation — missing). This
version would fail nearly every one of the final project's requirements,
not just one.

## The same example, improved

> **Problem.** Kestrel Pass Road (fictional) is a 4.2km rural two-lane
> stretch in NSW — the jurisdiction used throughout this proposal for cost
> and approval reference — bordered by continuous eucalypt scrub on both
> sides, with a traffic volume comparable to Coorabin Road in this unit's
> teaching dataset.
>
> **Evidence.** (i) *Physics* — Week 4's kinetic-energy relationship shows
> that higher speed means more energy involved in a collision, which is why
> this stretch's traffic speed is worth flagging at all; it does not, on
> its own, predict how severe any actual collision will be, since the real
> injury outcome also depends on the animal's mass, the geometry of the
> impact, and the vehicle's structure — the model explains why speed
> belongs in this discussion, not what any one collision will look like.
> (ii) *Ecology* — continuous roadside scrub without a break is the kind of
> pattern that makes it worth checking whether animals are actually
> crossing here in numbers. On its own it is a reason to survey movement on
> this stretch, not proof that this specific site has high crossing
> demand. (iii) *Engineering effectiveness* — Rytwinski et al. (2016), the
> meta-analysis introduced in Week 7, found fencing combined with a
> crossing structure reduced large-mammal road-kill by 83%, against roughly
> 1% for roadside reflectors and no detectable effect for crossing
> structures installed without fencing.
>
> **Mechanism.** Fencing physically excludes animals from the carriageway,
> and the crossing structure preserves the movement route the fencing would
> otherwise cut off. Running the fence the full 4.2km length of the
> continuous-scrub section, rather than stopping short partway along it,
> reduces — but does not by itself eliminate — the Week 7 "end of fence"
> displacement problem: the fence still has to end somewhere, so each
> endpoint needs its own check for animal movement and collision risk, with
> a plan (an angled return wing, extra signage, or a monitoring commitment)
> if that check finds a live risk there.
>
> **Intervention.** Exclusion fencing along the full 4.2km, plus one
> crossing structure, as the single primary measure. The midpoint is this
> proposal's *candidate* site for the crossing structure, not a confirmed
> one — final siting depends on an animal-movement survey, terrain, and
> engineering conditions that haven't been checked yet; the midpoint is
> where the investigation would start, not where construction is
> guaranteed to happen.
>
> **Implementation.** The figures below reuse Week 7's fictional
> planning-estimate assumptions for this teaching example — they are not a
> real NSW quote, and reusing them alone would not satisfy the final
> project's requirement for a realistic cost basis, which needs figures
> sourced from the actual road authority or a comparable real project.
> Illustrative only: approximately $756,000 initial cost and $25,200 annual
> maintenance. On approval: which authority signs off depends on how this
> stretch is classified — Transport for NSW is the roads authority for the
> state-road network, while regional and local roads sit with the relevant
> council — and that classification hasn't been confirmed for this
> fictional road. Fencing and vegetation work along a continuous scrub
> corridor may also need a Part 5 environmental assessment and a
> threatened-species check if the corridor turns out to support a listed
> species; both would need to be confirmed with the relevant authority
> before this pathway is anything more than "to be confirmed."
>
> **Evaluation.** A process metric (crossing-structure usage recorded via
> the same camera-trap method used in this unit's detection weeks) and an
> outcome metric (exposure-adjusted collision rate on this stretch,
> compared pre- and post-construction using the Week 3 adjustment method,
> not a raw count).

Notice what this version does that the weak version didn't: it uses only
*one* primary measure, names a real jurisdiction for cost/approval
purposes while keeping the road itself clearly fictional, separates "did
people build it and do animals use it" from "did collisions actually
fall" — and says out loud, next to each claim, which parts are backed by
evidence and which are still open questions (the exact crossing site, the
responsible authority, whether an environmental assessment is triggered,
a real-world cost). Writing "further investigation needed" where that's
genuinely true is stronger evidence practice than dressing an unconfirmed
detail up as settled just to make the proposal look finished.

## Handling a question you didn't anticipate

If asked something your proposal doesn't cover, say what your proposal
does and doesn't claim, rather than improvising an answer that oversteps
your evidence — "my proposal doesn't test that; here's what would be needed
to find out" is a stronger answer than a confident guess.

## What the in-class session does and doesn't do

This week's session gives you a chance to talk through your proposal with
peers and get feedback before submission. It adds no separate grade of its
own — marking is holistic, against the written proposal you submit by the
real deadline, per the [final project brief](/assessments/final-project/).
