The minutes right after a strike are not the time to work out reporting
obligations or figure out who pays — they're the time to keep everyone
present, human and animal, from getting a second injury. This lecture is
about exactly that window: what an untrained driver can safely and usefully
do, what they shouldn't attempt, and how to describe what they saw well
enough for someone trained to act on it.

## Safety first, always, before anything gets recorded

Before any note is taken, any photo is considered, or any animal is
approached, the first and only initial question is whether the scene itself
is safe: is the vehicle positioned off the traffic lane, are hazard lights
on, is anyone hurt. Nothing else in this lecture matters if that isn't
settled first.

## Three real tasks — not four

An untrained bystander at a strike scene has exactly three things they can
usefully do:

1. **ensure safety** — get people out of traffic, use hazard lights, don't
   create a second collision;
2. **contact the right service** — the right call depends on what's actually
   present at the scene (see the table below);
3. **give useful information** — a clear, honest account of what's there.

There is no fourth task. Treating the animal, restraining it, or moving it
is not something this lecture teaches, and it isn't something an untrained
person should attempt — that boundary is explained below, not softened.

## Useful information means observation, not guesswork

"Useful information" has a specific shape: a location description precise
enough to find the spot again (road name, direction of travel, a landmark
or distance from a known point — not "somewhere on the highway"), the
number of animals if known, the species if you're confident of it, and the
animal's condition *as observed*, not as guessed. "It didn't move after I
looked" is an observation. "It's probably fine, it hopped off eventually" is
a guess dressed as an observation, and the distinction matters to whoever
responds next.

## What this lecture does not tell you to do

Do not approach an injured or distressed wild animal to get a better photo
or confirm its species. That instruction is not softened by good intentions
— getting closer for identification purposes is exactly the situation where
an untrained person is most likely to get hurt, and it doesn't change what a
trained responder needs to know (which is closer to "large, grey, looked
badly injured" than a confirmed species anyway).

Large macropods, snakes, monitor lizards (goannas), bats and flying foxes,
koalas, and birds of prey are the categories most consistently flagged in
wildlife-rescue safety guidance as ones an untrained person should not try
to catch or handle — the risk runs in both directions, from bites and
scratches to disease exposure to stress-induced injury to the animal itself.
This lecture teaches what to observe and who to call. It does not teach
handling, and treats that as a hard boundary rather than a judgement call
for the person at the scene.

## For the human side: DRSABCD, named, not reproduced

St John Australia publishes a first-aid framework for human casualties —
**DRSABCD**: Danger, Response, Send for help, Airway, Breathing, CPR,
Defibrillation. This lecture names that framework so you know it exists and
what it stands for; it does not reproduce the steps, because St John
themselves are explicit that their fact sheet "is not a substitute for
first aid training" — the same caution applies here with more force, since a
lecture slide is a worse substitute for training than St John's own written
fact sheet is. A serious injury, a need for urgent medical help, or an
ongoing danger to a person is a 000 call before anything else in this
lecture applies — the same threshold as the contact table below, not every
instance of a person being present at the scene.

## A real contact-resource table

| Situation | Who to contact | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Life-threatening danger, a serious injury, or an urgent ongoing hazard — someone is badly hurt, there's a fire risk, or a dangerous traffic obstruction is still live | **000** (Police / Fire / Ambulance) | Reserve 000 for this tier — a minor scrape or a report you can safely make once things are stable doesn't need it |
| Wildlife rescue advice, anywhere in Australia | **WIRES: 1300 094 737** | 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, per [WIRES' emergency rescue advice page](https://www.wires.org.au/wildlife-rescue/emergency-rescue-advice); this line gives rescue *advice* over the phone — it is not itself a promise that a rescuer is standing by in every state, and WIRES states other contact channels, including social media, "are not monitored for rescues" |
| Your region's actual on-the-ground rescue organisation | Look it up via [WIRES' state-by-state rescue contact list](https://www.wires.org.au/national-wildlife-rescue) (Wildlife Victoria, RSPCA, Wildcare, and others, depending on where you are) | Call WIRES first for advice on what to do right now; use this list separately to find who can actually attend at your location — the two are not the same service |

## Legal obligation, organisational process, and general advice are three different things

Not everything in a rescue organisation's public guidance is a legal
requirement, and not everything a road authority asks for is what a
wildlife-rescue group needs — this lecture keeps those three categories
(what the law requires, what an organisation's process asks for, and what is
simply sensible general advice) visibly separate rather than blending them
into one undifferentiated "what to do" list.

## Where this goes next

Everything in this lecture assumes the person receiving your report can act
on it — which only works if what you tell them is actually clear under
stress. Week 10 is about designing communication for exactly that kind of
reader: someone who has to act fast, without local context, and possibly in
their second language.
