> "Welcome to Vehicular Macropod Avoidance. I had originally planned to teach
> this course theoretically." — Dr Kargaroo, opening remarks

Dr Kargaroo's qualifications include a doctorate in ecology and, in his own
words, "an unusually comprehensive incident report." He opens the semester by
putting that report to its first test: does one professor's collision prove
that a particular stretch of highway is unusually dangerous? Working through
why the answer is "not on its own" sets up everything the unit expects from a
claim about the problem.

## Why kangaroos, why now

That question is the one the rest of the semester answers piece by piece:

> How can we reduce kangaroo–vehicle collisions, and how would we know
> whether an intervention actually works?

Every later week — the ecology, the dataset, the physics, the engineering,
the policy — is one part of building toward it.

## One collision, several perspectives

The same collision reads differently depending on who is asked: the kangaroo
(movement, habitat, survival), the driver (detection, judgement, reaction),
the road authority (design and budget), the wildlife carer (welfare and
response), and the researcher (whether the data actually supports a
conclusion). Who gets to define whether a road is safe is not obvious, and
the lecture doesn't settle it for you — the road, for what it's worth,
received excellent reviews from everyone surveyed, but the kangaroos were
never on the mailing list.

## A convincing story is not yet convincing evidence

A single case can raise a good question; on its own, it can't establish how
often something happens or why. A road with more recorded incidents might be
more dangerous, or it might just be better monitored. "This road is
dangerous" needs to say, to whom, under what conditions, and measured
against what. Being in the dataset, as Dr Kargaroo puts it, does not
automatically qualify you to interpret it.

## What would we need to know?

Working outward from a single collision, the lecture lists what a proper
account would need: when and where it happened, the road and its
surroundings, what the vehicle and the animal were doing, the visibility
conditions, and what actually got recorded versus what didn't. Four ideas
recur for the rest of the semester — risk, exposure, outcome measures, and
uncertainty — introduced here as plain concepts, not vocabulary to memorise
yet.

## From blame to a research question

The lecture rewrites three familiar verdicts as testable questions instead:
"kangaroos just jump around" becomes a question about which conditions
predict roadside presence; "more warning signs would help" becomes a
question about a before-and-after comparison with everything else held
constant; "the new device obviously works" becomes a question about what
conditions it was tested under and what it actually measured. The skill this
unit is after is producing a question that could turn out to be wrong, not
arriving with the answer already chosen.

## The semester ahead

The course moves from understanding the problem, to reading and weighing
evidence, to analysing candidate interventions, to proposing something
testable. The two assessed pieces sit on that same arc: the week 6 literature
review and hotspot analysis (40%) establishes what the existing evidence can
actually support, and the week 12 research proposal (60%) puts forward an
intervention along with a way of judging whether it worked. You are not
required to solve road safety in twelve weeks — you are required to explain
how you would test your idea.
