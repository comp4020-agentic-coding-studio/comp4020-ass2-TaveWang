"Who's liable for a kangaroo strike" sounds like one question. It's actually
four, and casual conversation collapses them into each other constantly:
*who's at fault*, *what does insurance cover*, *who's contractually liable*,
and *what does it actually cost*. Each has a different answer, decided by a
different document, and a proposal that treats them as one question will
misread all four. This lecture works through each in turn, using one
jurisdiction — New South Wales — as the running example throughout, because
liability rules for roads authorities are not uniform across Australia and a
proposal has to name which one it's arguing under.

## Property damage and personal injury are answered by different schemes

| Aspect | Property damage | Personal injury |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical cover | Comprehensive motor insurance | Compulsory third-party (CTP) scheme |
| What it responds to | Damage to the insured vehicle | Injury to a person, not property |
| Animal-strike treatment | Depends on the policy's own clauses — not a universal rule; a policy has to spell out fault classification and excess itself | CTP schemes vary by state in what they require to establish how an injury occurred |
| What it doesn't cover | CTP does not cover vehicle damage | Comprehensive cover does not cover injury compensation |

These are not two flavours of the same claim — they are two entirely
separate schemes, triggered by different facts, and a strike that damages a
vehicle and injures a passenger produces two claims under two different
policies, assessed against two different sets of rules.

## Coverage, exclusion, excess and evidence

Whether a direct animal strike counts as "not-at-fault," what excess
applies, and whether it protects a driver's no-claim discount are not
settled by the fact that an animal was involved — they're settled by
whatever the policy's own clauses on claim classification, excess, and
discount protection actually say, and those vary between insurers and
policies. Reading which clause actually applies to what happened, rather
than assuming "animal was involved" activates one uniform kind of cover,
is the single most useful habit this lecture can give you. Below are two
fictional clauses from the same fictional policy, doing two different
jobs: one decides *which cover applies*, the other decides *what that
means for fault and excess*. Treating the first as if it answers the
second is a common and avoidable mistake.

**Fictional teaching contract excerpt — not a real insurer's policy:**

> *Comprehensive Motor Policy, cl. 4.2 (Animal Strike).* We will pay for loss
> or damage to Your Vehicle caused by a direct collision with an animal,
> provided the Vehicle was being driven in accordance with this policy at
> the time. This cover does not extend to damage arising from an attempt to
> avoid an animal that results in loss of control, which is covered under
> cl. 4.1 (Single Vehicle Accident) instead.
>
> *Comprehensive Motor Policy, cl. 4.3 (Claim Classification).* Where a loss
> is assessed as falling under cl. 4.2 (Animal Strike), the claim is
> classified as not-at-fault for no-claim-discount purposes and the
> standard excess of $500 applies, unless Our assessment finds that You
> failed to take reasonable care in the circumstances, in which case cl.
> 5.1 (At-Fault Excess) applies instead.

Notice what these clauses actually do between them: cl. 4.2 decides
whether an event is an Animal Strike loss at all ("hit the animal" versus
"swerved and crashed avoiding the animal" are the *same event* from a
road-safety perspective and *different clauses* from a claims
perspective); cl. 4.3 is the separate clause that then decides fault
classification and excess *for* an Animal Strike loss. A policy without
something like cl. 4.3 would leave those two questions genuinely
unanswered by cl. 4.2 alone — which is exactly the gap a careless reading
papers over.

## Rental vehicles change the document, not the physics

A rental agreement is not a comprehensive motor policy wearing a different
name — it's usually a separate damage-waiver arrangement with its own
excess, its own exclusions, and its own claims process, often administered
by the rental company rather than an insurer the driver chose. A driver's
own personal comprehensive policy frequently does not extend to a vehicle
they don't own, which means the rental company's own paperwork, not the
driver's usual insurer, is the document that actually governs a strike in a
hired car.

**Fictional teaching rental agreement excerpt — not a real rental
company's terms:**

> *Rental Vehicle Agreement, cl. 9 (Damage Waiver).* Subject to payment of
> the Damage Waiver Fee, Our waiver of Your liability for damage to the
> Vehicle applies with an excess of $2,750 per incident, except where the
> damage results from reckless or negligent driving, driving outside the
> permitted road types listed in Schedule 2, or a breach of any other term
> of this Agreement, in any of which cases the waiver does not apply and
> You are liable for the full cost of repair.

Reading this the same way as cl. 4.2 above: it tells you the waiver applies
*unless* one of the listed exceptions is met — it doesn't, by itself, tell
you whether swerving to avoid an animal counts as "reckless or negligent
driving." That's a factual judgement the rental company's assessor makes
from the specifics of what happened, not something the clause decides in
advance.

## Road authority liability: the actual-knowledge standard

Whether a road authority itself can be liable for *not* fencing or signing a
known hotspot is governed, in New South Wales, by the Civil Liability Act
2002 (NSW), s45 — "Special non-feasance protection for roads authorities."
Under that section, a roads authority is not liable for failing to carry
out, or to consider carrying out, road work unless it had *actual
knowledge* of the particular risk that caused the harm. General awareness
that kangaroos exist in a region is not the same as actual knowledge that
*this specific stretch* carries an elevated risk — the standard is
particular, not general. This rule only exists in its current form because
of Brodie v Singleton Shire Council [2001] HCA 29, the High Court decision
that abolished the older blanket "nonfeasance immunity" road authorities
used to enjoy and made something closer to ordinary negligence principles
apply to them in the first place — s45 is the (narrower) protection
Parliament wrote back in afterward. Both the wording of the standard and
the fact that it is one state's statute, not a national rule, matter for
anything you argue from it.

## Three fictional cases

**Case A — Own vehicle, direct strike.** A driver hits a kangaroo on a rural
road in their own, comprehensively insured vehicle. Significant vehicle
damage, no other vehicle involved. The direct collision plausibly falls
under cl. 4.2 (Animal Strike) above — but cl. 4.2 only decides *which*
cover applies, not fault or excess. Nothing in the facts given suggests the
driver failed to take reasonable care, so cl. 4.3 above points to a
not-at-fault classification and the standard $500 excess. That conclusion
comes from reading cl. 4.2 *and* cl. 4.3 together, not from cl. 4.2 alone.

**Case B — Rental vehicle, single-vehicle accident.** A visiting driver in a
rental car swerves at an animal, loses control, and damages the vehicle
without striking anything. This is governed by the rental agreement's cl.
9 (Damage Waiver) above, not the driver's personal policy — they may not
have one that extends to a hired car at all. Swerving to avoid an animal
and losing control isn't, on these facts alone, the same thing as
"reckless or negligent driving," so cl. 9's waiver plausibly still applies,
subject to its $2,750 excess — but that's the rental company assessor's
call to make from the specifics, not something this clause settles by
itself.

**Case C — Alleged authority knowledge.** A driver argues the road authority
"must have known" about a well-known hotspot on a specific stretch, and that
failing to install fencing or signage there was negligent. No record exists
showing the authority had *particular* knowledge of that stretch — only that
kangaroos are common in the region generally. Under the s45 standard as
described above, general regional awareness is unlikely to meet the
actual-knowledge threshold; this is illustrative reasoning about how the
test works, not legal advice about how any real dispute would resolve, and
the rule described is specific to NSW.

## What claims data does and doesn't tell you

An insurer's claims record is a record of who chose to lodge a claim, not a
census of every strike that occurred — the same reporting-bias logic Week 3
applied to police and ranger records applies here too, for a different
reason (an owner might not claim on a minor scrape to protect a no-claim
discount, or might claim on a rental precisely because it isn't their own
excess). Treating claims volume as a direct proxy for true incidence repeats
the exposure-adjustment mistake this unit keeps returning to, just wearing
an insurance-industry data source instead of a ranger-patrol one.
