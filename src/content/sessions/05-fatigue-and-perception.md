## Before the session

Read this week's lecture, especially the four-scenario table and the
distance-margin formula — you'll use both directly. Bring a calculator or a
laptop with a spreadsheet, as in week 4. No new dataset or download is
needed for this session.

## The four scenarios

This session works entirely from the same four **invented, illustrative**
scenarios the lecture introduces — not measured effects of fatigue, glare or
dusk, just labelled inputs built to isolate one variable at a time. All four
use a 1,500 kg vehicle and 6.5 m/s² braking deceleration, matching week 4.

| Scenario | Speed | Detection distance | Reaction time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 60 km/h | 70 m | 1.5 s |
| B | 80 km/h | 70 m | 1.5 s |
| C | 80 km/h | 50 m | 1.5 s |
| D | 80 km/h | 70 m | 2.0 s |

> reaction distance = speed × reaction time
> braking distance = speed² ÷ (2 × 6.5)
> stopping distance = reaction distance + braking distance
> distance margin = detection distance − stopping distance

## Two scene cards

This session's image exercise uses two written **scene cards** instead of
photographs — no roadside photography exists for this unit, and a real
photograph without a calibrated reference can't establish an actual
detection distance either, so a described scene serves the same discussion
purpose honestly. Treat both as prompts for discussion, not as a
reaction-time test, and don't try to back-calculate a real detection
distance from either description — neither is calibrated to a real distance
or a real light level.

**Scene card 1 — the open paddock verge.** A two-lane sealed road runs
straight for several hundred metres, bordered by open, grazed paddock on
both sides. The light is low and orange — the sun has just gone below the
horizon. A kangaroo stands side-on at the road's edge, roughly forty metres
ahead, its grey-brown coat against pale, dry grass. No oncoming traffic.

**Scene card 2 — the scrub cutting.** The same road enters a shallow rock
cutting lined with dense roadside scrub on the passenger side. The light
level is similar to scene 1. A kangaroo is partly behind a low bush, only
its head and one ear visible above the scrub line, at a similar distance
from the road edge. An oncoming vehicle's headlights are visible around the
next bend.

## In the session

This runs as one 50-minute sequence, after the short introductory lecture.
Work in the same pairs as previous weeks.

1. **5 minutes — predict.** Without calculating anything, each pair predicts
   which of the four scenarios has the largest distance margin, and writes
   one sentence saying why.
2. **10 minutes — calculate.** Calculate reaction distance, braking
   distance, stopping distance and distance margin for all four scenarios,
   showing your working for at least two of them.
3. **10 minutes — isolate a variable.** Pick two scenario pairs that each
   change only one input (for example, A→B changes only speed; B→C changes
   only detection distance; B→D changes only reaction time). For each pair,
   write one sentence stating what changed and by how much the margin moved.
4. **10 minutes — scene cards.** Look at both scene cards above. For each,
   write two sentences: one naming a factor from the lecture (contrast,
   glare, vegetation, attention) that could make the kangaroo harder to
   detect in that scene, and one naming what would have to be true about the
   driver or the road for detection to happen earlier.
5. **10 minutes — redesign.** Choose one scenario and change exactly one
   stated assumption (for example, a longer assumed detection distance, a
   lower speed, or a shorter reaction time). Recalculate its distance
   margin and write one sentence explaining what improved and why.
6. **5 minutes — submit.** Write one sentence stating your overall
   conclusion from the four scenarios, and one sentence naming a limitation
   of this exercise.

## Afterwards

This session's output is the **Visibility Budget Memo** — a one-page pair
submission. It's **formative and ungraded**: it's practice at using the
distance-margin calculation and naming its limits, not a mark on which
scenario you found scariest. It does not introduce any new assessed
requirement of its own.

**The Visibility Budget Memo**

- Your full calculations for all four scenarios (reaction distance, braking
  distance, stopping distance, distance margin), with units shown.
- One controlled comparison from step 3: which two scenarios, what changed,
  and by how much the margin moved.
- Your redesigned scenario from step 5: the changed assumption and the new
  margin.
- Two limitations of the exercise — for example, that an assumed detection
  distance isn't a measured one, or that a positive margin doesn't guarantee
  a safe outcome.

Keep your memo. Next week's technology discussion asks where a real
detection-distance number would actually have to come from, and what it
would take to trust one.
