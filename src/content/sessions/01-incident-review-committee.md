## Before the session

Read the week 1 lecture. Bring one popular-media claim about
kangaroo–vehicle collisions you've encountered — a news story, a road sign, a
rental-car pamphlet — that you suspect oversimplifies.

## In the session

You're convened as the Incident Review Committee for a single collision: the
Yarralong Road case. **This case, and everyone in it, is fictional teaching
material invented for this session — not a real incident, real person, or
real location.**

### The incident briefing

At approximately 6:40pm on a weekday in late autumn, a sedan travelling the
Yarralong Road — a regional two-lane road with an 80 km/h limit, no dedicated
wildlife fencing, and intermittent tree cover along both verges — struck an
adult kangaroo that entered the road from the eastern verge. The driver
braked and swerved but did not avoid the animal. The driver stopped, called
it in, and waited for a wildlife carer to attend. No other vehicles were
involved. The nearest wildlife-crossing sign is approximately 400 metres
further along the road, past the collision site.

### Driver's statement

> "I was doing about 80, maybe a bit under — it's hard to say exactly. I
> didn't have my eyes off the road. It came out of the trees on the left,
> and there just wasn't time. I've driven this road every week for two years
> and never seen one that close to the road before, but I don't really look
> for them, if that makes sense."

*(The driver's speed estimate is not backed by any independent record —
there is no fixed camera or data logger on this stretch. Treat it as a
recollection, not a measurement.)*

### Wildlife carer's notes

> Attended 7:05pm. Adult female eastern grey kangaroo, no pouch young
> observed. Injuries consistent with vehicle impact; deceased on arrival.
> Site is unfenced verge with moderate tree cover extending roughly
> 15m back from the road. No tag or prior record for this individual. Unable
> to determine how long the animal had been in the immediate area before the
> collision, or whether this stretch has a prior collision history — that
> would need to come from the road authority's own records, which I don't
> have access to from the field.

### Five role cards

- **Researcher** — Your stake: whether any general claim can be drawn from
  this one case. You know how to ask what would need to be true for a
  pattern (not just an instance) to exist. You do not know the collision
  history of this specific road, or how representative this case is of
  anything.
- **Road authority** — Your stake: whether the existing signage and any
  future spending decision is defensible. You know the sign placement (400m
  past the site) and the general design of the road. You do not have, in
  front of you today, the road's collision history — the carer's notes flag
  this gap deliberately.
- **Driver** — Your stake: whether your account is treated as fact or as one
  input among several. You can report what you remember seeing and doing.
  You do not have any way to independently verify your own speed or
  reaction time.
- **Wildlife carer** — Your stake: the animal's welfare and an accurate
  clinical record. You know the animal's condition, species, and that its
  injuries are consistent with vehicle impact. You do not know anything
  about traffic volume, signage history, or the driver's account beyond
  what's in this briefing.
- **Kangaroo representative** — Your stake: that the animal's perspective
  (habitat, movement, why this verge, why this time of day) isn't skipped
  in favour of blaming the driver. You hold the session's one standing
  objection: *"Before we discuss my decision-making, could we discuss why
  the road is here?"* — raise it before the group settles on blame.

1. **10 minutes** — sort the briefing, statement, and notes into established
   fact, someone's interpretation, and genuinely unknown.
2. **15 minutes** — each role states the question they most want answered.
3. **15 minutes** — the group agrees on one research question, one
   falsifiable hypothesis derived from it, and three pieces of missing data
   that would help test it.
4. **10 minutes** — report back: check whether the media claim you brought
   would survive the committee's own standard of evidence, and name one
   conclusion the group still can't draw from this case alone.

## Afterwards

Submit a one-page Incident-to-Question Brief. It's formative practice for
turning an account into a testable claim, not a graded artefact. Fill in:

**Incident-to-Question Brief**

- Research question the case raises: **\_\_\_\_**
- Hypothesis derived from that question (must be specific enough that
  evidence could contradict it): **\_\_\_\_**
- Three established facts from the case material: **1. \_\_\_\_ 2. \_\_\_\_
  3. \_\_\_\_**
- Three pieces of missing information that would help test the hypothesis:
  **1. \_\_\_\_ 2. \_\_\_\_ 3. \_\_\_\_**
- One conclusion this case does not yet support: **\_\_\_\_**
- One source of evidence that could move it forward: **\_\_\_\_**

This session doesn't require the shared collision dataset — that's
introduced properly in week 2, where the Road Ecology Research Group's
synthetic collision log replaces this one fictional case with a much larger
set of cases. You'll leave today with a partner for the week 3 mapping
exercise.
