## Before the session

Read the syllabus and the two papers linked from the opening lecture. Bring
one popular-media claim about kangaroo–vehicle collisions you've encountered
— a news story, a road sign, a rental-car pamphlet — that you suspect
oversimplifies.

## In the session

You're convened as the Incident Review Committee for a single collision: a
car and a kangaroo, one stretch of road, a driver's statement, a few notes
from the wildlife carer who responded, and several details nobody wrote
down. The case is a fictional teaching scenario, clearly marked as such on
the handout, not a real incident.

Groups take on five roles: researcher, road authority, driver, wildlife
carer, and kangaroo. The kangaroo representative holds the session's one
standing objection — *"Before we discuss my decision-making, could we
discuss why the road is here?"* — which exists to keep the group from
settling on blame before anyone has looked at the infrastructure.

1. **10 minutes** — sort the case material into established fact, someone's
   interpretation, and genuinely unknown.
2. **15 minutes** — each role states the question they most want answered.
3. **15 minutes** — the group agrees on one research question and three
   pieces of missing data that would help answer it.
4. **10 minutes** — report back: check whether the media claim you brought
   would survive the committee's own standard of evidence, and name one
   conclusion the group still can't draw.

## Afterwards

Submit a one-page Incident-to-Question Brief: your research question, three
established facts, three missing pieces of information, one conclusion the
case doesn't yet support, and one source of evidence that could move it
forward. It's formative practice for turning an account into a question, not
a graded artefact.

You also leave with access to the shared collision dataset — which the week
2 session assumes you already have — and a partner for the week 3 mapping
exercise.
