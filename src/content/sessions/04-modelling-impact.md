## Before the session

A short lecture runs first this week, covering the kinetic-energy and
stopping-distance equations and a worked example, so you have both formulas
and one worked calculation before the session asks you to run the rest
yourself. Bring a calculator or a laptop with a spreadsheet — no physics
background and no programming are required.

## The scenario

Every calculation this week uses the same **synthetic teaching scenario**
from the lecture:

| Given | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Vehicle mass | 1,500 kg |
| Speeds | 40, 60, 80 km/h |
| Reaction time (assumption) | 1.5 s |
| Braking deceleration (assumption) | 6.5 m/s² |

Treat every figure above as an illustrative input, not a measured value or a
safe-driving recommendation.

## Calculation worksheet

Fill in one row per speed. The formulas:

> speed (m/s) = speed (km/h) ÷ 3.6
> kinetic energy (J) = ½ × mass × speed (m/s)²
> reaction distance (m) = speed (m/s) × reaction time
> braking distance (m) = speed (m/s)² ÷ (2 × deceleration)
> total stopping distance (m) = reaction distance + braking distance

## In the session

This runs as one 50-minute sequence, after the short lecture. Work in the
same pairs as previous weeks.

1. **5 minutes — predict.** Before calculating anything, write one sentence
   predicting what happens to kinetic energy when speed doubles (40 to 80
   km/h). No looking back at the lecture slides yet.
2. **10 minutes — convert and calculate energy.** Convert all three
   scenario speeds to metres per second, then calculate kinetic energy for
   each using the 1,500 kg mass. Fill in the first three columns of the
   worksheet above.
3. **10 minutes — stopping distance.** Calculate reaction distance,
   braking distance and total stopping distance for each speed, using the
   stated assumptions. Fill in the remaining columns.
4. **10 minutes — plot.** Produce two simple graphs from your six numbers:
   speed against kinetic energy, and speed against total stopping
   distance. Graph paper, a spreadsheet chart, or a careful hand-drawn plot
   are all acceptable.
5. **10 minutes — change one assumption.** Pick either reaction time or
   deceleration and change it to a different, clearly stated value (for
   example, a 2.0 s reaction time for a distracted driver, or a = 5.0 m/s²
   for a wet road). Recalculate total stopping distance at all three
   speeds and compare the two sets of results.
6. **5 minutes — explain.** In plain language, with no equations, write one
   sentence explaining one result from your worksheet, and one sentence
   naming a limitation of the model you just used.

## Afterwards

This session's output is the **Physics Does Not Negotiate Worksheet** — a
one-page pair submission. It's **formative and ungraded**: it's practice at
running a simplified model and stating its limits, not a mark on your
arithmetic. It may support how you interpret the physics half of the week 6
assignment, but it does not introduce any new assessed requirement of its
own.

**The Physics Does Not Negotiate Worksheet**

- Your stated assumptions (mass, reaction time, deceleration — including
  whichever one you changed in step 5).
- Your full worksheet, all six calculations, with units shown at every
  step.
- Your two graphs (speed against energy, speed against stopping distance).
- One written comparison between two of the three speeds (for example, what
  changed and by how much between 40 and 80 km/h).
- Two limitations of the model, in plain language — drawn from the
  lecture's list or one you noticed yourself.

Keep your worksheet. It's a rehearsal for the kind of quantitative reasoning
the week 6 assignment expects you to show your working for.
