# Week 6

## Roo bars, AEB, and their blind spots

### The Brochure Has Excellent Daytime Vision

---

## A polished claim

> "Our system detects wildlife before you do."

Which wildlife? At what distance? In what lighting? With what consequence?

A claim that answers none of these hasn't told you anything yet.

```notes
Let the room sit with the claim for a moment before moving to the
four-function breakdown — the vagueness is the point.
```

---

## Four different functions

- **detection** — identifies a possible hazard
- **warning** — alerts the driver
- **automatic emergency braking (AEB)** — changes the vehicle's motion
- **impact protection** — changes the collision, if one occurs

A roo bar is impact protection only. It does not detect. It does not brake.

---

## False positives, false negatives

100 staged trials: 40 with an animal present, 60 without.

| Trial | Detected | Missed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Animal present (40) | 34 | 6 |

| Trial | False alarm | Correctly quiet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No animal (60) | 9 | 51 |

False negative rate = 6/40 = **15%**. False positive rate = 9/60 = **15%**.

Different denominators. A single "accuracy" figure hides both.

---

## Detection distance meets the margin

> Distance margin = assumed detection distance − modelled stopping distance

- 120 m stated range, 80 km/h (71.3 m stopping): margin **+48.7 m**
- same system, 60 m once obscured by vegetation: margin **−11.3 m**

The headline range and the fine-print range are not the same claim.

---

## Detection rate ≠ collision prevention

85% detection is not 85% of collisions prevented.

Between detection and prevention: warning comprehension, driver reaction,
whether the vehicle can actually stop in time, whether the animal stays
put.

One is a claim about a sensor. The other is a claim about an entire chain.

---

## Performance varies by condition

- **lighting** — daylight, dusk, night, glare
- **animal type and posture** — stationary and broadside vs. fast and end-on
- **obstruction** — vegetation hides animals from sensors too
- **speed** — validated at 60 km/h isn't validated at 100 km/h

A card that states one condition has told you about one condition.

---

## Three cards and a roo bar

| Card | Function | Range/distance | Latency | Unverified for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A — optical | detect + warn | 90 m daylight, degrades below ~20 lux | 0.4 s | low light |
| B — thermal | detect + warn | 120 m, ~60 m if obscured | 0.6 s | reptiles, small animals |
| C — AEB fusion | detect + brake | TTC-triggered, to 60 km/h | 0.3 s | wildlife, >60 km/h |
| Roo bar | impact protection | n/a | n/a | compatibility, injury outcomes |

## Designing a fair test

A test can favour a product without lying — just by choosing conditions.

A fair test states conditions in advance, includes the ones a vendor would
rather skip, reports a real denominator for every rate, and says plainly
what it didn't test.

```notes
This is the actual skill for the session: spotting which of these a given
test skipped.
```

---

## Insufficient evidence is a valid answer

None of the three fictional systems has been shown to prevent a collision.

Each has been shown to do something narrower, under stated conditions.

"Conditionally promising" is an honest ceiling. You are not required to
pick a winner.
