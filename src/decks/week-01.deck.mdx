## Why kangaroos, why now

One professor's collision doesn't prove a road is dangerous. It does raise
the question the whole semester exists to answer:

> How can we reduce kangaroo–vehicle collisions, and how would we know
> whether an intervention actually works?

---

## Who gets to define "dangerous"?

- the kangaroo — movement, habitat, survival
- the driver — detection, judgement, reaction
- the road authority — design and budget
- the wildlife carer — welfare and response
- the researcher — whether the data backs the conclusion

The road received excellent reviews from everyone surveyed. The kangaroos
were never on the mailing list.

---

## A convincing story is not yet convincing evidence

- a case can raise a question; it can't on its own establish a rate
- more recorded incidents might mean more incidents — or just better records
- "dangerous" needs to say: to whom, under what conditions, measured how

Being in the dataset does not automatically qualify you to interpret it.

---

## Before drawing a conclusion, we'd want to know

- when and where it happened
- the road and its surroundings
- what the vehicle and the animal were doing
- the visibility conditions
- what got recorded, and what didn't

Four words that keep coming back all semester: **risk, exposure, outcome,
uncertainty.**

---

## Turning a verdict into a question

- "kangaroos just jump around" → which conditions predict roadside presence?
- "more signs would help" → compared against what, with what else held
  constant?
- "the new device obviously works" → tested under what conditions, measuring
  what?

---

## Where this goes

1. understand the problem
2. read and weigh the evidence
3. analyse candidate interventions
4. propose something testable

- **week 6** — literature review and hotspot analysis (40%)
- **week 12** — research proposal for a safety intervention (60%)

You are not required to solve road safety in twelve weeks. You are required
to explain how you would test your idea.
